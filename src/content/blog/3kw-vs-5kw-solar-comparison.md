## What the number on the inverter actually means

"3kW" and "5kW" refer to the inverter's continuous output capacity — the total load it can run **at the same time**, not the size of your house or your monthly bill. A 3kW inverter can supply 3,000 watts continuously; a 5kW inverter can supply 5,000. Go over that limit, and the inverter throttles, trips, or shuts down to protect itself — it doesn't gracefully "borrow" extra capacity from anywhere.

This is the number that decides everything else in this comparison: what you can run simultaneously, how much you'll pay upfront, and how much battery makes sense to pair with it.

## What a 3kW inverter is built for

A 3kW system is the right fit for a smaller household running the essentials — lighting, a fridge, a TV, phone and laptop charging, a router, maybe a small water pump used briefly. It comfortably covers everyday life for a couple, a small family, or a compact home office, especially where heavy appliances like electric cookers or washing machines aren't expected to run off solar.

Where 3kW gets tight is when you try to stack loads — running an iron box while the fridge cycles on and the pump kicks in can push you close to or past the ceiling. It's not that 3kW is weak; it's that everything running "at once" adds up faster than people expect.

## What a 5kW inverter unlocks

Step up to 5kW, and the picture changes: you can run a washing machine, a microwave, and general household loads at the same time without flinching. It's the size most installers recommend for a standard family home that wants more breathing room — not just "enough," but enough with headroom for the appliances people tend to add over time (a second fridge, a bigger TV, an extra pump for irrigation).

5kW is also where hybrid systems start to make sense for small businesses — shops, salons, small offices — where multiple pieces of equipment run concurrently through the day rather than one at a time.

## The actual price difference

This is where the decision gets real. Based on current inverter pricing in the Kenyan market, a 5kW-class hybrid inverter typically runs somewhere in the KSh 140,000–200,000 range on its own, while a comparable 3kW unit is usually 30–40% cheaper — often landing in the KSh 90,000–130,000 range, depending on brand and features like Wi-Fi monitoring or dual MPPT.

That's the number that actually matters when you're comparing full packages, not just nameplates.

## Battery sizing: the part people skip

Inverter capacity tells you what you can run *at once*. Battery capacity tells you *how long* you can run it. A 3kW system typically pairs well with a 5–7 kWh battery for solid overnight coverage of essentials. A 5kW system, to actually justify its higher simultaneous-load ceiling, generally wants 8–12 kWh of storage — otherwise you have the muscle to run heavy loads but not the stamina to do it for long after sunset.

This is exactly why complete kits (inverter, battery, and panels sold together) tend to make more sense than buying an inverter alone and guessing at the rest — the components are pre-matched to actually work as a system rather than three separate purchases that don't quite fit together.

## Real household scenarios

**A young couple in a Nairobi apartment**, running lights, a fridge, a TV, and charging electronics, rarely touching a washing machine or heavy cooking appliance — 3kW comfortably covers this with room to spare, and the savings on the smaller inverter and battery are real money back in your pocket. This is also the profile where a 3kW system pays itself off fastest, simply because the household was never going to stress the higher ceiling anyway.

**A family of five in a standalone home**, running a fridge, multiple TVs, a washing machine on weekends, an electric kettle daily, and occasional ironing — this is squarely 5kW territory. Trying to force this household onto 3kW usually means constant load management: waiting to run the kettle until the washing machine is off, unplugging the iron before switching on the microwave. It works, technically, but it turns solar into a daily puzzle instead of the convenience it's supposed to be.

**A small shop or salon** running lighting, a fridge for drinks, hair dryers or small equipment, and a laptop or till system through the day — 5kW gives enough headroom that staff aren't thinking about what's plugged in before they switch something on, which matters more than it sounds when you're trying to run a business rather than babysit an inverter.

## Common mistakes when choosing between them

The biggest one is **sizing for today's bill, not today's actual usage**. People look at their current electricity bill and assume it maps directly to inverter size, but a low bill sometimes just means the household already limits appliance use out of habit — running the kettle less, avoiding the iron during peak hours, delaying laundry — habits that tend to relax once solar backup removes the anxiety about outages and rising tariffs.

The second is **buying 5kW inverter capacity with 3kW-sized batteries and panels**, assuming the bigger number is simply "better." Without the storage and generation to match, that extra inverter capacity sits unused most of the time — money spent on a ceiling you never actually hit, while the real bottleneck (a small battery running flat by 9pm) goes unaddressed.

The third is **ignoring future appliances**. If you're planning to add a second fridge, an AC unit, or expect the household to grow, sizing a little above your current exact needs — rather than exactly to them — saves you from a frustrating, costly upgrade in two years, when you'd otherwise need to replace the inverter entirely rather than just adding a panel or two.

## So which one should you get?

If your home runs on the basics — lighting, a fridge, a TV, electronics, and the occasional short-burst appliance — a 3kW system is genuinely enough, and paying for 5kW would mostly be paying for capacity you won't use. If your household regularly runs a washing machine, kettle, microwave, and general loads at overlapping times, or you're equipping a small business with multiple simultaneous devices, 5kW is where you stop fighting your own inverter and start just living normally.

The honest answer for most standard family homes in Kenya, though, leans toward 5kW — not because 3kW is inadequate, but because the jump in comfort and headroom is often worth the price difference once you account for how households actually behave once solar removes the fear of blackouts. People use more, not less, once reliable power stops feeling scarce.
