## Start with your actual consumption, not a guess

The single biggest mistake people make is sizing a system around what they *think* they use, or worse, around what a neighbor bought. Every home's load profile is different — a family running a fridge, three phones, and evening lighting needs a fraction of what a home running a borehole pump and an electric iron needs.

The right starting point is your electricity bill. Kenya Power bills show your consumption in kWh for the month. Divide that by 30 and you have your rough daily average. That single number is the foundation everything else is built on.

![Electricity meter and bill](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1581093588401-fbb62a02f120?w=1200&q=80)

## How much system do you actually need?

Once you know your daily consumption in kWh, sizing follows a fairly predictable formula:

**System size (kWp) = Daily energy need (kWh) ÷ (peak sun hours × system efficiency)**

In Nairobi and most of Kenya, peak sun hours average around 4.5–5, and system efficiency (accounting for inverter losses, wiring, dust, and temperature) typically sits around 75–80%. So a home consuming 10 kWh a day would need roughly a 3 kWp system to cover it comfortably.

## The four components that make up your system

**Solar panels** capture sunlight and convert it to DC electricity. Monocrystalline panels dominate the residential market now — higher efficiency per square meter than older polycrystalline options, which matters if your roof space is limited.

![Solar panels close up](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1613665813446-82a78c468a1d?w=600&q=80)

**The inverter** is the component doing the real work behind the scenes. It converts DC power from your panels into the AC power your appliances actually use, and in a hybrid setup, it also manages how power flows between solar, battery, and grid. A cheap inverter is the single most common point of failure in underperforming systems — it's not the part to cut corners on.

![Inverter and battery system](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1624378439575-d8705ad7ae80?w=600&q=80)

**Batteries** store excess daytime generation for use at night or during outages. Lithium-ion has largely replaced lead-acid for new installations: it handles deeper discharge cycles, needs far less maintenance, and lasts significantly longer, even though the upfront cost is higher.

**Mounting and balance-of-system** — the racking, cabling, breakers, and earthing — rarely gets discussed, but poor mounting is why systems fail during Kenya's heavier rain and wind seasons. This is also where corners get cut on cheap installs, and where problems show up two or three years later, not on day one.

<div class="callout">
  <strong>A note on oversizing:</strong> bigger isn't automatically better. An oversized system means paying for panels and batteries that sit underused most days. Right-sizing to your actual load, with a small buffer for future appliances, is almost always the more sensible investment.
</div>

## Off-grid, hybrid, or grid-tied — which one fits you?

Not every home needs the same setup, and this choice affects both cost and what happens during an outage.

**Grid-tied** systems have no battery — they generate power during the day and feed straight into your home's consumption, cutting your Kenya Power bill but offering zero backup when the grid goes down. This is the cheapest entry point and suits homes with reliable grid power that mainly want to reduce monthly costs.

**Hybrid systems** are the most common choice for Kenyan homes right now. They combine panels, an inverter, and a battery, staying connected to the grid as backup while prioritizing solar and battery power first. You get both savings and blackout protection, at a mid-range cost.

**Off-grid systems** carry no grid connection at all — the battery bank has to cover every hour the sun isn't shining. These make sense for rural properties beyond the grid's reach, but they require more generous sizing and a larger battery investment to avoid running short on cloudy days.

For most homes in Nairobi, Kiambu, or similar urban and peri-urban areas, hybrid is the sweet spot: it protects you from token shortages without asking you to gamble your entire power supply on battery capacity alone.

These figures include panels, inverter, a modest battery bank, mounting, and installation labor. Battery capacity is usually the single biggest cost driver — if budget is tight, a grid-tied system without storage (or a smaller battery) can bring the price down significantly while still cutting your monthly bill.

## What installation day actually looks like

A typical residential install runs 1–3 days. Day one covers mounting the racking and panels, running conduit, and positioning the inverter and battery. Day two is wiring, earthing, and commissioning — this is when the installer tests the system under load and walks you through the monitoring app. For larger commercial jobs, add a few days for structural assessment and permitting.

![Solar installation team](https://images.unsplash.com/photo-1598614186910-7cb5d6a8cd66?w=1200&q=80)

## Maintenance is lighter than people expect

Solar systems are low-maintenance by design, but not zero-maintenance. Panels should be rinsed every few weeks in dusty areas — dust buildup can quietly cut output by 10–15% without any obvious sign. Inverters benefit from clear airflow around them; don't box them into a sealed cupboard. Lithium batteries need essentially no manual maintenance, but it's worth checking the monitoring app monthly to catch any drop in charge cycles early, before it becomes a bigger issue.

## Financing and getting paid for excess power

If upfront cost is the barrier, most Kenyan solar providers now offer installment plans, and some banks have started rolling out green-energy loan products with better terms than standard asset financing. For businesses generating more power than they use, Kenya Power's power purchase framework allows qualifying producers to feed excess generation back to the grid — worth exploring once you're past the residential scale.

## Mistakes worth avoiding

The most common one is chasing the cheapest quote without checking what's actually in it — inverter brand, battery chemistry, and warranty terms vary enormously between quotes that look similar on paper at first glance. Two "5kWp systems" priced 200,000 shillings apart are rarely equal; one is usually cutting corners on the inverter or using a shorter-lived battery chemistry to hit that lower number.

The second is skipping a proper site assessment. A quote given over the phone, without anyone seeing your roof orientation, shading from neighboring buildings or trees, or your actual appliance load, is a guess dressed up as a design. Roof angle and shading in particular can quietly cut real-world output well below the manufacturer's rated figures, and no phone call can catch that.

The third is ignoring warranty and after-sales support entirely. A panel with a 25-year warranty is only worth something if the company issuing it is still trading in five years, let alone twenty-five — ask how long the installer has been operating locally, and whether they carry stock for common repairs, before signing anything.

## Where to go from here

Sizing a solar system doesn't need to feel like guesswork. Start with your actual consumption, understand what each component is doing and why it costs what it costs, and treat any quote that skips a site visit with healthy skepticism. The calculator above will get you in the right neighborhood — a proper assessment will get you the exact number.
