Here is something that happens in almost every solar consultation in Kenya. The homeowner asks how much it costs. The installer gives a number. The homeowner writes it down, thanks them, and leaves — without any way to know whether that number is reasonable, inflated, or quietly missing three components that will need to be purchased later anyway.

This guide changes that. The prices below are drawn from current market data across Nairobi suppliers in 2026 — not ranges so wide they are useless, but specific figures you can hold a quote against. By the end you will know what each component costs on its own, what a complete installed system should cost at four common sizes, what legitimately drives prices up, and the one question that separates a serious installer from one who is not.

## 💡 What you are actually buying — and what each piece costs

Most people think of solar as one purchase. It is actually five separate components, each with its own price range, its own quality spectrum, and its own lifespan to budget for. Understanding what each one costs individually is what lets you read a bundled quote critically.

## 💡 The VAT situation — what is actually exempt

<cite index="22-1">VAT is currently zero-rated on most solar equipment in Kenya, which meaningfully reduces your total installation cost compared to other capital purchases.</cite> In practice this means solar panels, certain inverters, and batteries classified as solar storage equipment are zero-rated — you pay 0% VAT rather than the standard 16%.

<div class="callout-orange">
  <span class="callout-icon">⚠️</span>
  <div>
    <strong>One important development to watch in 2026:</strong> <cite index="34-1">The Finance Bill 2026 may reclassify solar batteries from zero-rated to exempt status, which could raise consumer prices through trapped input VAT.</cite> This has not yet passed at the time of writing, but it is worth asking your installer whether their quote is based on current VAT status and whether a reclassification would affect the price.
  </div>
</div>

What the exemption means in practice: a complete 5kW system that would cost KSh 780,000 with standard VAT applied to all components costs closer to KSh 650,000 with correct zero-rating. The saving is real and significant. An installer who adds 16% VAT across all line items without distinguishing which components qualify is either making an error or padding the quote.

## 💡 What drives the price up legitimately

Not every expensive quote is overpriced. Some sites genuinely cost more than others, for reasons that are completely valid.

<div class="price-driver-grid">
  <div class="driver-card">
    <div class="driver-icon">🏠</div>
    <strong>Roof type</strong>
    <p>Iron sheet is cheapest to mount on. Concrete slab, clay tile, and flat roofs need special hardware, additional labour, and sometimes a structural assessment. This can add KSh 30,000–80,000 to the installation line legitimately.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="driver-card">
    <div class="driver-icon">📏</div>
    <strong>Cable distance</strong>
    <p>If your battery cabinet is far from the distribution board — across a compound, through walls, or down multiple floors — cable costs increase significantly. A 25mm² DC cable run of 15 metres costs meaningfully more than 5 metres.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="driver-card">
    <div class="driver-icon">🌊</div>
    <strong>Coastal location</strong>
    <p>Mombasa and coastal installations require marine-grade components — corrosion-resistant mounting hardware, UV-stabilised conduit, sealed connectors. This adds 10–15% to component costs compared to a Nairobi install.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="driver-card">
    <div class="driver-icon">🏷️</div>
    <strong>Brand quality</strong>
    <p><cite index="30-1">Budget brands can be KSh 15,000–25,000 cheaper per panel, and KSh 40,000–80,000 cheaper on inverters, but for a system that lasts 20 years or more, investing in proven brands usually pays off.</cite> The warranty is only as good as the company honouring it locally.</p>

## 💡 EPRA licensing — what it means and why it matters

<cite index="36-1">Any company that designs, installs, sells, imports, or manufactures solar PV systems in Kenya must hold a valid EPRA Solar PV licence.</cite> This is not optional and it is not a formality. <cite index="36-1">EPRA has stepped up enforcement in 2026, and unlicensed work can lead to fines, court action, and seized equipment.</cite>

<div class="callout-red">
  <span class="callout-icon">💥</span>
  <div>
    <strong>What EPRA licensing means for you as a homeowner:</strong> an EPRA-licensed installer has passed a competency assessment, carries identifiable credentials, and can be held accountable by the regulator if their work falls below standard. An unlicensed installer has none of those things — and if something goes wrong with their installation, your recourse is limited to whatever you put in the contract, if there was one.
  </div>
</div>

Verifying a licence is straightforward: ask the installer for their EPRA licence number and check it on the EPRA public register at **epra.go.ke**. A legitimate installer will have this ready without hesitation. The licence class should match the work — a technician licence (T1, T2, T3) is for individual technicians, while a contractor licence (C1) covers companies offering installation services.

## The number behind the number


Solar is one of the few investments where the price you pay and the value you receive are almost entirely determined by how well the system was designed before anyone bought a single component. A correctly sized system with certified components, installed by an EPRA-licensed technician, will generate the savings it promised for twenty-five years. A system that was sized by guesswork, built with grey-market batteries, or installed by someone with no credentials will cost you again — in replacements, in underperformance, or in something more serious — before those twenty-five years are up.

The prices in this guide give you a baseline. What sits between that baseline and the quote in your hand is the difference between a system built to last and one built to sell.

<script>
  function updateCostChecklist() {
    const boxes = document.querySelectorAll('.quote-checklist input[type="checkbox"]');
    const total = boxes.length;
    let checked = 0;
    boxes.forEach(b => { if (b.checked) checked++; });
    document.getElementById('costCheckCount').textContent = checked + ' of ' + total + ' confirmed';
    const msg = document.getElementById('costCheckMsg');
    if (checked === total) {
      msg.textContent = "That is a complete, verifiable quote. You are in a strong position to compare and commit.";
    } else if (checked === 0) {
      msg.textContent = "A quote that cannot confirm these eight points is not a complete quote.";
    } else if (checked <= 4) {
      msg.textContent = "Getting there — the unticked items carry real financial risk and are worth pressing on before signing.";
    } else {
      msg.textContent = "Almost complete. The remaining gaps are still worth confirming — especially the EPRA licence and battery chemistry.";
    }
  }
  document.querySelectorAll('.quote-checklist input[type="checkbox"]').forEach(box => {
    box.addEventListener('change', updateCostChecklist);
  });
  updateCostChecklist();
</script>
