In May 2026, energy economists based in Nairobi issued a warning that barely made it past the business pages. A high-value home abroad had burned — the fire traced back to a solar installation. Their concern was specific: Kenya, a continental leader in solar adoption, had no reliable system for certifying the components being installed on its rooftops or the technicians putting them there. The gray market for inverters and panels was, in their words, perilously fractured.

The warning landed quietly. It probably should not have.

This is not a story about solar being dangerous. A properly designed, correctly installed solar system is one of the safest additions you can make to a home. But there is a significant gap between that sentence and the average Kenyan rooftop installation, and that gap has a specific shape. It is made of loose connectors, sealed battery boxes, overheating inverters, and bird droppings sitting on a single panel for six months. None of those things look dangerous. All of them can become dangerous, on a long enough timeline, in ways that move faster than anyone expects once they start.

## 💥 The thing nobody tells you about fire and solar

Most homeowners assume that if their solar system is working — if the lights are on, the monitoring app shows numbers, the inverter hums quietly — everything is fine. That assumption is correct most of the time. The problem is that the conditions that eventually cause a solar fire are almost entirely invisible during normal operation, and they develop slowly, across months and years, right up until they don't.

<div class="fire-stat-box">
  <span class="fire-emoji">🔥</span>
  <div>
    <strong>Faulty electrical connections are responsible for nearly 25% of all solar-related fires globally.</strong> The connection itself looks fine from the outside. The danger is in what is happening inside it, invisibly, every time current flows through a slightly imperfect joint.
  </div>
</div>

<img src="/images/solar-connector-close-up.jpg" alt="Close-up of solar panel MC4 connectors — the most common starting point for solar electrical fires" />

The fire does not start with the panel. It starts, almost always, somewhere in the electrical system between the panel and the wall — a connector creating resistance, a cable running too hot, an inverter working in a space it was never designed to ventilate, or a battery being charged in conditions that slowly push it past its safe limits. By the time there is smoke, the process has usually been underway for a while.

## 🔥 Hot spots: the danger hiding in plain sight

Here is something worth knowing about solar panels: shade is not just inefficiency. It is a potential heat source.

When part of a panel is shaded — by a fallen leaf, a bird dropping, a branch that grew through last rainy season — the shaded cells stop generating electricity and start acting as resistors. Current from the unshaded cells is forced through them, and that resistance converts directly to heat. A localized area of the panel overheats. This is called a hot spot, and it does not look like anything from the ground.

<div class="warning-box">
  <span class="fire-emoji">⚠️ 🔥</span>
  <strong>Hot spots tend to worsen over time.</strong> Heat concentrates in the affected area, damaging the cell further, which increases resistance, which generates more heat. A bird dropping from January can, by July, have created a hot spot severe enough to burn through the panel's backsheet — the layer between the cells and your roof.
</div>

<img src="/images/solar-panel-hot-spot-thermal.jpg" alt="Thermal imaging of a solar panel showing a hot spot — invisible to the eye but detectable with the right equipment" />

The fix is straightforward: keep panels clean. A soft cloth and water, every few weeks during the dry season, and after any storm that deposits debris. It costs nothing and eliminates one of the most common pathways to panel degradation and fire risk. The complication is that most Kenyan homeowners are never told this, and most installers do not follow up to check.

## 💥 The inverter: working hard in a box that doesn't breathe

Your inverter is converting electricity all day, every single day. That process generates heat — not dangerous heat, under normal conditions, but heat that has to go somewhere. Manufacturers design inverters with specific ventilation requirements: minimum clearance on each side, maximum ambient temperature, protection from direct sun exposure.

  <div class="info-card">
    <div class="info-icon">☀️</div>
    <div class="info-text">
      <strong>Direct sun exposure</strong> raises an inverter's operating temperature far beyond its design range. Nairobi temperatures in the dry season combined with a south-facing wall can push an inverter into thermal stress daily.

  </div>
  <div class="info-card">
    <div class="info-icon">🌫️</div>
    <div class="info-text">
      <strong>Dust accumulation</strong> inside the ventilation gaps acts as an insulating layer, trapping heat that the inverter's internal fans were designed to move out. In Kenya's dusty seasons, this builds faster than most people realise.
    </div>
  </div>
  <div class="info-card">
    <div class="info-icon">💧</div>
    <div class="info-text">
      <strong>Water intrusion</strong> from a poorly sealed outdoor installation causes internal corrosion and short circuits. Nairobi's rainy seasons are not kind to electronics mounted in positions the installer did not think through.
    </div>
  </div>
</div>

<img src="/images/solar-inverters.jpg" alt="Solar inverter mounted on an exterior wall — ventilation clearance and sun exposure are critical installation decisions" />

An inverter that overheats repeatedly does not fail immediately. It degrades. Components inside it slowly lose tolerance. And eventually, it fails in a way that is harder to predict and harder to control than a simple shutdown.

## 🔥🔥 Batteries: the part of the conversation that needs to change

If the inverter is the most misunderstood component in a solar system, the battery is the most underestimated hazard. This has become significantly more relevant as lithium-ion battery storage has become mainstream in Kenyan residential installations.

Lithium batteries store a remarkable amount of energy in a small space. That energy density is also what makes a lithium battery failure different from any other component failure in your system.

<div class="fire-stat-box danger">
  <span class="fire-emoji">🔥🔥</span>
  <div>
    <strong>When lithium batteries fail, the fires are often more serious and much harder to control than normal electrical fires.</strong> Battery fires can burn for hours and release toxic smoke. Standard fire suppression methods are frequently ineffective, and some fires reignite after appearing to be out.
  </div>
</div>

The conditions that push a lithium battery toward failure are not exotic. Overcharging — from a faulty charge controller or an inverter not properly configured for the battery chemistry — causes cells to swell. Physical damage from a poor installation or a fall does the same. Mixing old and new batteries in the same bank, or batteries from different manufacturers, creates imbalances that lead to individual cells overcharging even when the system appears to be running normally.

<div class="warning-box">
  <span class="fire-emoji">💥</span>
  <strong>A Battery Management System (BMS) is not optional for lithium batteries — it is the component that stands between normal operation and thermal runaway.</strong> A lithium battery installed without a functioning BMS, or with a BMS not properly matched to the battery chemistry, has no protection against the conditions that cause catastrophic failure. Ask specifically whether your installation includes one, and whether it has been configured for your battery type.
</div>

The other issue is physical location. Batteries generate heat during charging and discharging. A sealed cabinet with no airflow traps that heat, raising the operating temperature of every cycle. In direct sun — which some installations allow because nobody thought through the siting — the ambient temperature starts the cycle already elevated. Heat shortens battery lifespan under normal conditions. Under the wrong conditions, it shortens it in a more immediate way.

## 🔥 What a solar fire looks like — and why it moves faster than you expect

<img src="/images/fire-extinguisher-home-safety.jpg" alt="Home fire safety equipment — knowing where it is and how to use it matters more than most homeowners realise" />

A solar fire, once started, does not behave like a kitchen fire or a curtain catching a candle. The panels on your roof are live as long as there is daylight — there is no switch that removes the voltage. Firefighters responding to a solar property treat the entire roof as energised electrical equipment, which restricts what they can do and where they can safely work.

<div class="fire-stat-box">
  <span class="fire-emoji">🔥</span>
  <div>
    <strong>Solar panels continue generating electricity even during a fire.</strong> Water applied directly to live panels creates an electrocution risk for everyone nearby. The same system that was powering your home quietly becomes an active hazard for the people trying to protect it.
  </div>
</div>

This is not a reason to avoid solar. It is a reason to know where your main isolators are, to have them clearly labelled, and to make sure every adult in your household knows how to activate them. It is also a reason to have a working smoke detector inside any room where batteries or inverters are housed — not because disaster is likely, but because early warning makes every other response more manageable.

## The honest summary 💥

Solar fire risk is real, specific, and — in the majority of cases — entirely preventable. A German study of roughly 100,000 solar installations found a fire incident rate of around 0.006%. That figure is for systems installed to standard, maintained properly, and using certified components. It is a reassuringly low number.

The honest caveat for Kenya is that the installation standards, component certification systems, and post-installation inspection frameworks that produce that figure do not consistently exist here yet. Energy economists in Nairobi are not issuing warnings because solar is inherently dangerous. They are issuing them because the conditions that keep the risk that low elsewhere have not reliably been put in place.

A correct installation, with certified components, proper ventilation, a functioning BMS on the battery, and panels cleaned on a schedule — that system carries a risk low enough to ignore. The question worth asking is whether that description matches your roof.
