The most common mistake in Kenyan solar installations is not a wiring fault or a bad panel — it is a system that was never the right size to begin with. Too small and the batteries run flat by nine at night. Too large and you have spent KSh 200,000 more than necessary on capacity that sits idle. Either way, someone handed you a quote before properly counting what you actually use.

This guide fixes that. By the end of it you will know exactly what questions to ask, how to check whether a quote makes sense, and what a correctly sized system looks like across four common storage targets — 3kWh, 5kWh, 10kWh and 15kWh.

<img src="/images/sizing.jpg" alt="Kenya Power token meter on a Nairobi wall — your KPLC bill is the starting point for any accurate solar sizing calculation" />

## 💡 Start with one number: your daily kWh

Before anyone mentions panels, inverters or batteries, you need to know how much electricity your home actually uses in a day. There are two ways to find this.

<div class="method-grid">
  <div class="method-card">
    <div class="method-icon">📄</div>
    <strong>From your KPLC bill</strong>
    <p>Look at your monthly units (kWh) consumed. Divide by 30. That is your daily average. A typical Nairobi 3-bedroom home uses 8–15 kWh per day. A smaller 2-bedroom unit might use 4–8 kWh.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="method-card">
    <div class="method-icon">✏️</div>
    <strong>By counting appliances</strong>
    <p>List every appliance you want to run. Find its wattage (on the label or manual). Multiply watts × hours used per day = watt-hours. Add everything up and divide by 1,000 to get kWh.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="callout-orange">
  <span class="callout-icon">💡</span>
  <div>
    <strong>The number that matters most is not what you use now — it is what you want solar to cover.</strong> Some homeowners want full independence. Others want solar to cover nighttime loads only, topping up with KPLC during the day. That decision changes everything that follows.
  </div>
</div>


## 💡 The three numbers every system is built around

Once you know your daily kWh, three numbers flow from it. Understanding what each one does is what separates a homeowner who can read a quote critically from one who cannot.

<div class="three-grid">
  <div class="three-card blue">
    <div class="three-num">1</div>
    <strong>Battery capacity (kWh)</strong>
    <p>How much energy you store. Sized to cover your evening and overnight load — not your whole day. Most homes need 6–10 hours of storage at their overnight load.</p>
    <div class="three-formula">Evening load (kW) × Hours × 1.25 (losses) = kWh needed</div>
  </div>
  <div class="three-card amber">
    <div class="three-num">2</div>
    <strong>Inverter size (kVA)</strong>
    <p>How much power you can use at once. Sized to your peak simultaneous load — all the things you might run at the same time. Not your total — your peak moment.</p>
    <div class="three-formula">Total simultaneous watts + 25% margin = inverter kVA needed</div>
  </div>
  <div class="three-card green">
    <div class="three-num">3</div>
    <strong>Panel array (Watts)</strong>
    <p>How fast you recharge. Sized to refill your battery bank in a single day of good sun. Kenya averages 5.5 peak sun hours — use that as your divisor.</p>
    <div class="three-formula">Battery kWh ÷ 5.5 hrs ÷ 0.8 (efficiency) = array kW needed</div>
  </div>
</div>

## 💡💡 MPPT vs PWM — this is not a debate

Before going further, one component decision needs settling: the charge controller. This is the device that sits between your panels and your batteries, managing how power flows between them.

<div class="compare-box">
  <div class="compare-item bad">
    <div class="compare-label">PWM — Pulse Width Modulation</div>
    <p>The older, cheaper type. Works by clipping panel voltage down to match battery voltage — which means any panel voltage above the battery level is simply wasted. In a real Kenyan installation, <strong>PWM wastes 20–30% of what your panels generate</strong> every single day, for the entire life of the system.</p>
    <div class="compare-verdict bad-verdict">❌ Not suitable for battery storage systems</div>
  </div>
  <div class="compare-item good">
    <div class="compare-label">MPPT — Maximum Power Point Tracking</div>
    <p>The modern standard. Constantly hunts for the maximum power point your panels can deliver at that moment — adjusting for temperature, cloud cover, and time of day. Extracts 20–30% more usable energy from the same panels, every day, across a 25-year system life.</p>
    <div class="compare-verdict good-verdict">✅ Required for all modern systems</div>
  </div>
</div>

<cite index="26-1">The only acceptable choice for a modern system is an MPPT controller. The difference in price between MPPT and PWM is small compared to the efficiency gain.</cite> Over a 25-year system life, the energy a PWM controller wastes can represent a significant fraction of your original investment — spent panels generating power that never reaches a battery.

## 💡 System voltage: 24V or 48V?

Voltage is the part of a solar specification that confuses most people and gets explained the least. Here is the short version.

<div class="voltage-grid">
  <div class="voltage-card">
    <div class="voltage-badge v24">24V</div>
    <strong>For 3kWh systems</strong>
    <p>Appropriate for smaller systems where peak power is under 2,000–3,000W. 24V components are widely available in Kenya and well understood by most installers.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="voltage-card">
    <div class="voltage-badge v48">48V</div>
    <strong>For 5kWh and above</strong>
    <p>Higher voltage means lower current for the same power. Lower current means thinner cables, less heat loss, smaller fuses, and a safer installation. For anything above 3kWh, 48V is the right choice.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<div class="callout-blue">
  <span class="callout-icon">🔍</span>
  <div>
    <strong>Simple rule:</strong> if your inverter is 3kVA or above, it should be a 48V system. An installer quoting you a 5kVA inverter on a 24V system either does not understand the system or is selling you what they have in stock.
  </div>
</div>

## 💡 What every system needs regardless of size

<img src="/images/safety.jpg" alt="Solar safety components inside a properly built cabinet — isolators, surge protection, fusing and earthing are not optional at any system size" />

These four components appear in every correctly built system, at every storage size. They are the ones most often quietly removed when a quote needs to come down.

<div class="four-safety-grid">
  <div class="safety-item">
    <div class="safety-icon">⚡</div>
    <strong>Overcurrent protection</strong>
    <p>A DC fuse or breaker between the battery and the inverter, rated for DC interruption. AC breakers are not suitable for DC circuits and will not safely interrupt a DC fault.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="safety-item">
    <div class="safety-icon">🛡️</div>
    <strong>Surge protection device (SPD)</strong>
    <p>Absorbs voltage spikes from lightning and grid switching before they reach your inverter — which is typically the most expensive single component in the system.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="safety-item">
    <div class="safety-icon">🔌</div>
    <strong>DC and AC isolators</strong>
    <p>Physical switches that allow the system to be de-energised section by section. Required for safe maintenance, emergencies, and fire response. Must be clearly labelled.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="safety-item">
    <div class="safety-icon">🌍</div>
    <strong>Earthing</strong>
    <p>A physical connection to ground that gives fault current a safe path. Protects the person who touches a faulty casing, not just the equipment. Not negotiable at any system size.</p>
  </div>
</div>

## 💡 How to check if a quote makes sense

<cite index="29-1">A well-sized 5kW system serving a home with a KES 12,000 to KES 18,000 monthly Kenya Power bill typically pays back in 4 to 6 years, then runs largely free for the remaining 20-plus years.</cite>

That payback figure only holds if the system is correctly sized. An undersized system that cannot cover your actual load saves less than the calculation promises. An oversized system that cost more than necessary pushes payback further out.

<div class="quote-checklist">
  <h3>💡 Reading a solar quote — what to check</h3>
  <p class="quote-sub">Before you sign any quote, confirm these five things are explicitly stated in the document.</p>

  <div class="check-row">
    <input type="checkbox" id="q1" />
    <label for="q1">The battery capacity is stated in kWh — not just Ah. (200Ah at 12V = 2.4kWh. 200Ah at 48V = 9.6kWh. The voltage matters enormously.)</label>
  </div>
  <div class="check-row">
    <input type="checkbox" id="q2" />
    <label for="q2">The charge controller is specified as MPPT — not PWM, and not just "solar charge controller"</label>
  </div>
  <div class="check-row">
    <input type="checkbox" id="q3" />
    <label for="q3">The inverter kVA rating is at least 25% above your total simultaneous appliance load</label>
  </div>
  <div class="check-row">
    <input type="checkbox" id="q4" />
    <label for="q4">Cable gauge is specified for the DC runs — not just "cables included"</label>
  </div>
  <div class="check-row">
    <input type="checkbox" id="q5" />
    <label for="q5">Surge protection, isolators, and earthing are itemised separately — not bundled into "accessories"</label>
  </div>
  <div class="check-row">
    <input type="checkbox" id="q6" />
    <label for="q6">The battery chemistry is specified — LiFePO4 (lithium iron phosphate) and lead-acid are not interchangeable, and their real costs over a system life are very different</label>

## The number your installer should produce


At the end of a proper solar consultation, your installer should be able to hand you a document that shows your daily kWh load, the battery bank sized to cover your overnight hours, the inverter sized to your peak simultaneous load, the array sized to recharge that battery in a day, and the safety components included in each of those figures.

<cite index="27-1">Inverter size is determined primarily by your peak power demand. If the total demand from your appliances exceeds this limit, the inverter will overload and either shut down or reduce performance to protect the system.</cite>

If the conversation never involved your KPLC bill, never counted your appliances, and went straight to a panel count and a price — that is not a sizing consultation. That is a sales call. The two are not the same thing, and the difference usually shows up two years after installation when the batteries run flat at nine in the evening and nobody answers the phone.

A correct solar system, sized from your actual daily load and installed with all four safety components in place, is one of the most reliable energy investments available in Kenya right now. The panels that went on a roof in Nairobi in 2000 are still generating power today. The question is simply whether what is underneath them was built to the same standard.

If you are not certain, we are happy to start from the numbers — your KPLC bill, your appliances, and what you actually want solar to cover — before anything else gets discussed.

<script>
  function updateQuoteChecklist() {
    const boxes = document.querySelectorAll('.quote-checklist input[type="checkbox"]');
    const total = boxes.length;
    let checked = 0;
    boxes.forEach(b => { if (b.checked) checked++; });
    document.getElementById('quoteCheckCount').textContent = checked + ' of ' + total + ' confirmed';
    const msg = document.getElementById('quoteCheckMsg');
    if (checked === total) {
      msg.textContent = "That is a complete, reviewable quote. You are in a strong position to compare and decide.";
    } else if (checked === 0) {
      msg.textContent = "A quote that cannot answer these questions is not a complete quote.";
    } else if (checked <= 3) {
      msg.textContent = "Getting there — the unticked ones are worth going back to ask about before signing.";
    } else {
      msg.textContent = "Almost complete. The remaining gaps are still worth confirming before you commit.";
    }
  }
  document.querySelectorAll('.quote-checklist input[type="checkbox"]').forEach(box => {
    box.addEventListener('change', updateQuoteChecklist);
  });
  updateQuoteChecklist();
</script>
