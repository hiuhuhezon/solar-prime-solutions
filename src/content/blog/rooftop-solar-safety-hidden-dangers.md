There is a moment in most solar consultations where the rooftop stops being a rooftop and becomes a platform. A surface to maximise panel coverage on. Installers talk about orientation, tilt angle, shading analysis, how many kilowatts will fit. What they rarely talk about — at least not in detail, at least not unbidden — is what happens to the roof itself once the panels go on.

Or what happens to the person putting them there.

This blog is about those two conversations. Not because solar is dangerous to install or to live with — when it is done correctly it is neither — but because "done correctly" requires someone in the room knowing what correct looks like before the first hole gets drilled.

<img src="/images/solar-detailed.jpg" alt="A solar installation worker on a Nairobi rooftop — fall hazards are the leading cause of serious injury in the solar installation industry" />

## 💥 The worker on your roof

Here is a fact that rarely comes up in the sales conversation: <cite index="45-1">solar installation workers are vulnerable to the highest rates of fatal falls in the construction industry.</cite> Falls are, globally, the leading cause of work-related deaths in construction — and rooftop solar installation puts workers directly into that category, often without the harnesses, anchor points or fall arrest systems that regulated industries require.

<div class="danger-box">
  <span class="icon">💥</span>
  <div>
    <strong>In Nairobi specifically, 65% of all construction site injuries result from workers falling from heights or being struck by falling objects.</strong> Kenya currently has no specific regulations governing working at height — meaning the safety standard on your roof on installation day depends almost entirely on what the company you hired decided to bring with them.
  </div>
</div>

<img src="/images/rooftop-fall-protection-harness.jpg" alt="Proper rooftop fall protection harness and anchor system — what a compliant solar installation crew should arrive with" />

This matters to you as a homeowner for a reason that goes beyond the obvious moral one. If a worker is injured on your property during an installation — falling from your roof, through a skylight, or off a ladder — the question of who bears responsibility is not always simple. A company without workers' compensation insurance, installing without safety equipment, on a residential property: that is a set of facts that can become your problem as quickly as theirs.

<div class="info-box blue">
  <span class="icon">🔍</span>
  <div>
    <strong>Before any installer climbs your roof, ask two questions:</strong> Does your company carry workers' compensation insurance for rooftop installation work? And what fall protection equipment will your crew use on the day? A serious installer will answer both without hesitation. An installer who cannot answer either is telling you something important about what else they may have skipped.
  </div>
</div>

## 🔥 Every hole in your roof is a potential leak

Mounting solar panels on most rooftops requires drilling. Racking brackets are anchored into the roof structure — ideally into the rafters — and each penetration point becomes, permanently, a place where water can enter if it is not sealed correctly.

<cite index="38-1">Roof leaks from solar installations typically result from improper penetration sealing and flashing installation errors. Every mounting point creates a potential water entry path, and inadequate sealing around these penetrations allows water infiltration that can cause extensive structural damage over time.</cite>

<img src="/images/solar-roof-mounting-flashing.jpg" alt="Solar panel roof mounting bracket with flashing — every penetration point requires proper sealing to prevent water damage" />

The challenge is specific to Nairobi's climate in a way that matters. The long rains arrive in April. The short rains follow in October. A mounting point that was sealed adequately — but not correctly — may hold through the first season and the second. By the third, the sealant has expanded and contracted through enough temperature cycles that the seal is no longer what it was. Water finds the gap. It enters slowly, invisibly, tracking along the roof structure until it appears as a stain on a ceiling board or — if it gets that far without being noticed — as rot in the timber below.

<div class="warning-box">
  <span class="icon">⚠️ 💥</span>
  <div>
    <strong>Many installers lack proper roofing experience.</strong> They know how to mount panels. They do not always know how to flash a penetration correctly, how to select sealant appropriate for iron sheet versus concrete tile, or how thermal expansion affects a seal over time. Those are roofing skills, not solar skills — and the industry does not consistently require both from the same person.
  </div>
</div>

<img src="/images/roof-leak-water-damage-interior.jpg" alt="Water damage on ceiling from roof penetration leak — one of the most common consequences of improperly sealed solar mounts" />

The standard to look for: every mounting bracket should be flashed — a metal sleeve that directs water away from the penetration — and sealed with a product rated for the specific roof material. Butyl tape, silicone sealant, and purpose-made roof penetration boots are not interchangeable. An installer who uses whatever was in the van is making a decision about your ceiling on your behalf.

## 💥💥 Your roof may not be ready for what solar weighs

<cite index="36-1">Solar panels add about 2.5 to 4 pounds per square foot with mounting hardware.</cite> That is not a large number on its own. Spread across a structurally sound roof with adequate rafter spacing and no pre-existing damage, a standard residential system sits within what the structure was designed to carry.

The issue is that structurally sound is doing a lot of work in that sentence.

<div class="weight-grid">
  <div class="weight-card">
    <div class="weight-icon">🏚️</div>
    <strong>Aging roofs</strong>
    <p>A roof more than 15 years old may have timber that has absorbed moisture, developed minor rot, or lost structural integrity in ways invisible from the surface. Solar adds load to a structure that is already past its design baseline.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="weight-card">
    <div class="weight-icon">🔩</div>
    <strong>Poor weight distribution</strong>
    <p>Mounting systems that concentrate load on a small number of anchor points create stress on individual rafters rather than distributing it across the structure. Sagging, cracking, and eventual structural failure follow the same path.</p>
  </div>
  <div class="weight-card">
    <div class="weight-icon">🌧️</div>
    <strong>Drainage disruption</strong>
    <p>Panels installed without accounting for roof drainage can pool water at their edges. In iron sheet installations common across Kenya, standing water accelerates corrosion at exactly the points where mounting hardware has already disturbed the roof surface.</p>
  </div>
</div>

<img src="/images/roof-structure-rafter-inspection.jpg" alt="Roof rafter inspection before solar installation — structural assessment is a mandatory step that many Kenyan installers skip" />

<cite index="42-1">Solar systems typically last 25 to 30 years, so the roof should have enough remaining life to support the solar panel installation.</cite> That is a sensible starting point. If your roof is ten years old and your panels are rated for twenty-five more, you are looking at a replacement at some point in the middle of your system's life — which means removing and reinstalling every panel, re-doing every mounting point, and absorbing that cost inside what was supposed to be a clean energy investment.

A structural assessment before installation is not a luxury item. It is the step that determines whether everything that follows is being built on something solid.

## 🔥 The inspection nobody schedules

There is a roof inspection that happens before installation — or should. And there is a different roof inspection that should happen periodically after it, which almost nobody schedules because nobody tells them to.

<div class="info-box orange">
  <span class="icon">🔥</span>
  <div>
    <strong>Post-installation, your roof changes in ways you cannot see from inside the house.</strong> Mounting bolts can loosen as materials expand and contract through seasons. Sealant degrades. Galvanic corrosion develops where dissimilar metals contact each other at mounting points. The panels cover the areas most at risk, which means those areas are also the least visible during any casual inspection.
  </div>
</div>

<img src="/images/blog/solar-panel-roof-inspection-drone.jpg" alt="Roof inspection of solar installation — thermal imaging and visual inspection from above can identify issues invisible from the ground or inside" />

The standard recommendation from structural and roofing professionals is a visual inspection of all mounting points and flashings every two years, with particular attention after any severe weather event. In Nairobi's context — where April and October reliably deliver heavy rainfall, where daytime temperatures in Machakos, Thika, and the wider basin regularly exceed 30°C, and where temperature swings between day and night are significant — that two-year interval is a reasonable minimum rather than a conservative one.

What to look for, or have someone look for: rust staining around mounting bolts, sealant that has pulled away from the bracket, tiles or iron sheets that have shifted around a mounting point, or any new staining on the ceiling directly below a bracket location.

## The roof does not come with the warranty

Solar panel warranties run to twenty-five years. Inverter warranties run to ten. What you will not find in any of those documents is a warranty on what happens to the roof the panels are sitting on — because that is not the panel manufacturer's responsibility. It is the installer's, and only to the extent that the contract you signed made it so.

<cite index="44-1">Rooftop solar systems are generally safe when they are properly designed, installed, inspected, and maintained. Most incidents involving fires, roof leakages, or structural damage result from poor workmanship, substandard materials, inadequate maintenance, or failure to comply with established engineering standards rather than from solar technology itself.</cite>

That sentence contains its own checklist. Properly designed. Correctly installed. Inspected. Maintained. The solar industry in Kenya is growing at 15% annually — fast enough that the supply of installers who can honestly claim all four of those things has not kept pace with the number who claim them anyway.

The questions in the checklist above are not suspicious questions. They are the questions a careful homeowner asks, and the ones a careful installer expects. Any installer who finds them unreasonable is, once again, giving you the most useful information available before the money changes hands.
