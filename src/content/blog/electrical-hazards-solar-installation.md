There is a detail about solar electricity that most installers mention once, quietly, near the end of a long conversation about kilowatts and payback periods — and that most homeowners promptly forget because it doesn't feel urgent at the time.

Your solar panels cannot be switched off.

Not while the sun is up. Not if you flip the inverter breaker, not if you cut the main switch, not if you disconnect every isolator in the cabinet. As long as light is hitting those panels, they are generating direct current electricity — silently, continuously, with no indicator light and no warning sound. A standard residential string in Kenya running twelve panels produces somewhere between 400 and 600 volts DC on a clear afternoon. That is not a figure that leaves room for a second chance.

This is the starting point for understanding electrical hazards in solar installation — not to make you afraid of your own roof, but because knowing what you're actually dealing with changes the questions you ask, the installers you hire, and the warning signs you know to look for.

## DC and AC are not the same danger

Every socket in your house runs on alternating current. AC electricity cycles — it rises, falls, crosses zero, rises again, fifty times every second. That zero-crossing is not just a technical detail. It is what allows fuses and breakers to interrupt the circuit cleanly, what allows your body some chance of recovery from a brief contact, and what allows an electrical arc to extinguish itself when the fault clears.

Direct current does none of those things. It flows in one direction, continuously, at constant voltage. <cite index="18-1">DC arcs are much harder to extinguish because the current never naturally drops to zero. This can lead to a greater release of energy at the fault point.</cite> In practical terms: a DC arc that forms inside a connector, a junction box, or a poorly terminated cable does not go out on its own. It sustains itself. It intensifies. It generates temperatures that can reach several thousand degrees at the fault point — hot enough to ignite cable insulation, roof timbers, and anything stored nearby — often with no visible warning until the damage is already done.

<cite index="7-1">DC arc faults are one of the more serious hidden risks. Direct current does not stop the same way AC power does, so once an arc starts, it is harder to control. The heat keeps building inside rooftops, walls, or inverter spaces, and often there is no clear warning before the damage gets serious.</cite>

This is why the wiring quality inside a solar installation is not a cosmetic detail. It is a fire risk that operates invisibly, behind conduit and cabinet doors, for years.

## The connector nobody checks

<img src="/images/mc4-connector-solar.png" alt="MC4 solar connectors — the most common point of failure in residential solar installations" />

The connectors joining your panels to each other and to the rest of the system are called MC4 connectors. They are small, black, and completely unremarkable looking. They are also one of the most common failure points in any residential installation.

<cite index="7-1">A lot of electrical fire causes start with faulty DC connectors, especially MC4 connectors. If they are loose, badly installed, or mixed with incompatible parts from different manufacturers, they begin to create resistance and heat. At first, it looks like a small issue. Over time, that heat keeps building and can lead to arcing or even ignition.</cite>

The problem is partly that MC4 connectors from different manufacturers are not fully interchangeable, even when they look identical. A connector from Brand A mated with a connector from Brand B creates an imperfect seal that allows moisture in and lets heat build. The other problem is that they are on your roof, hidden inside cable runs, and nobody is looking at them. The degradation happens slowly, over months or years, until it doesn't.

A proper installation uses matched connectors throughout, fully seats every connection, and routes cables so they are protected from UV exposure and mechanical damage. The difference in component cost between doing this correctly and cutting corners is negligible. The difference in outcome, years later, is not.

<div class="callout-box">
  <strong>Ask your installer this:</strong> "Are all MC4 connectors in this installation from the same manufacturer, and are they rated for my system's maximum current?" A qualified technician will have that answer immediately. Hesitation is worth noting.
</div>

## Why switching off doesn't switch off

<cite index="19-1">Solar panels generate DC electricity whenever exposed to light. Unlike AC electricity, which crosses zero 50–60 times per second, DC is continuous — it doesn't self-extinguish when a fault occurs. There is no way to stop a solar panel generating voltage while it is exposed to light. Covering with an opaque tarp reduces but doesn't eliminate output; night or complete darkness is the only true shutdown.</cite>

This creates a specific hazard for anyone who assumes that flipping the isolator makes the system safe to touch. It does not. The isolator breaks the circuit between the panels and the inverter, which is necessary and important — but the cable between the panels and the isolator itself remains live. On a bright Nairobi afternoon, that cable is carrying several hundred volts DC, and it will continue doing so until the sun goes down.

<cite index="13-1">DC shocks can cause delayed cardiac effects. Get medical attention even if they seem fine.</cite> An AC shock at the same voltage often throws a person clear of the contact point — the muscular spasm is violent but it breaks contact. DC does the opposite: it causes sustained muscular contraction, meaning the person grips tighter rather than pulling away. The contact is held longer. The consequences are correspondingly worse.

This is not an argument against solar. It is an argument for understanding exactly what your system contains and making sure the person who installed it understood the same.

## What a rushed installation looks like, years later

<img src="/images/solar-wiring-cabinet-kenya.png" alt="Inside a solar cabinet — proper installation includes labelled isolators, surge protection, and correctly sized cabling throughout" />

Kenya imported 495 MW of solar panels in the twelve months to June 2026, up from 200 MW in 2021. That is a 147 percent increase in four years. The demand for installers has grown faster than the supply of well-trained ones, and the gap between what a careful installation looks like and what a rushed one looks like is not always visible from the outside.

The common shortcuts that create electrical hazards are not dramatic — they are small decisions that compound over time:

**Undersized cabling** is cheap to hide and expensive to discover. Every cable has a current rating. Running a cable at or above its rated capacity means it runs warm under load, the insulation degrades faster, and eventually the failure is not a question of if but when.

**Missing or incorrectly rated overcurrent protection** means that when a fault does occur, there is nothing in the circuit designed to interrupt it safely. The fault runs until something gives.

**Poor earthing** is the one that protects you rather than the equipment. A proper earth connection gives fault current a safe path. Without it, that path may be the person standing next to the cabinet touching a casing that is not supposed to be live.

**No surge protection** leaves your inverter — typically the most expensive single component in the system — exposed to the voltage spikes that Kenya's grid delivers regularly, and that nearby lightning generates without warning.

None of these look like anything from the outside. The system works. The lights come on. The monitoring app shows generation figures. And somewhere behind a locked cabinet door, a shortcut from the installation day sits quietly in the wiring.

## The three questions that tell you whether to be concerned

You do not need to become an electrician to protect your household from a poorly installed system. You need to be willing to ask three questions before signing off on any installation, and to treat vague answers as a signal worth following up on.

**First:** "Can you show me the DC and AC isolators, and explain how to use them in an emergency?" These should be clearly labelled, accessible without tools, and the technician should be able to walk you through the sequence without hesitation.

**Second:** "Is there arc fault protection in this system?" Arc fault circuit interrupters (AFCIs) are increasingly standard in well-specified systems precisely because DC arc faults are the leading electrical fire cause in solar installations. Not every system has them. You should know whether yours does.

**Third:** "What earth connection does this installation use, and where is it?" Earthing is not negotiable. An installation without it is not complete, regardless of how well everything else looks.

<div class="callout-box">
  <strong>One thing to remember after dark:</strong> Night is the only time your panels are genuinely de-energised. If you ever need to access any part of the DC wiring — even to check a connection — wait until well after sunset, and still do not do it without a qualified technician present.
</div>

## The system works until it doesn't

Solar energy is not dangerous in the way a gas leak is dangerous — immediately detectable, immediately urgent. The electrical hazards in a poorly installed solar system build quietly, inside conduit and behind cabinet doors, across months and years of normal operation. The system generates power throughout. Nothing indicates that anything is wrong.

That is precisely what makes the quality of the initial installation so consequential. There is no inspection that happens automatically after the installer leaves. There is no annual check required by anyone. There is only what was done on the day, and whether the person who did it knew the difference between a system that works and a system that was built to last safely.

If you are considering solar for the first time, or you have an existing system that was installed without much conversation about what was going into your wall, those three questions are a fair place to start. A good installer will welcome them. And any installer who doesn't is giving you exactly the information you need.
